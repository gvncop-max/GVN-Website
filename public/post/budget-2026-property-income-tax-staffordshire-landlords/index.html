Budget 2026 Landlord Tax Rise: What Staffordshire Landlords Pay

By Valentine Grey15 November 20267 min read

Four percentage points. That is how much more tax a landlord who owns property in their own name will pay on rental profit from April 2027, compared with today. Half of it was announced a year ago. The other half arrived in the Budget on 28 October 2026, and it landed on top.

The short answer: from 6 April 2027, rental profit will be taxed at 24%, 44% or 49%, not 20%, 40% or 45%. The relief for mortgage interest is tied to the new 24% rate. Landlords who own through a limited company are not affected by this measure. For a typical Stoke-on-Trent let, the change is measured in hundreds of pounds a year, not thousands.

Key points

  • Budget 2026 adds 2 percentage points to every property income tax rate, on top of the 2 points set by Budget 2025.
  • From the 2027 to 2028 tax year: property basic rate 24%, higher rate 44%, additional rate 49%.
  • The rates still need to pass in Finance Bill 2026-27. The law today says 22%, 42% and 47%.
  • HMRC estimates around 5% of taxpayers will pay on average £225 a year more in 2027 to 2028 from this change alone.
  • The personal allowance (£12,570) and basic rate band (£37,700) are frozen until April 2031.

What did Budget 2026 change for landlords?

It raised the tax rates on rental profit again. The Budget 2026 document (opens in a new tab) puts it plainly: “Budget 2025 increased the rates of tax on savings and property income by two percentage points across all tax bands from April 2027. The government will increase these rates by a further two percentage points from that date.”

HMRC’s policy paper, published on 29 October (opens in a new tab), sets out who is caught: “individuals and partnerships who receive income from letting property”, plus trustees and personal representatives, in England, Wales and Northern Ireland.

Band2026 to 2027 (now)2027 to 2028 (Budget 2026)Extra tax per £1,000 of profit
Basic rate20%24%£40
Higher rate40%44%£40
Additional rate45%49%£40

One honest caveat. The Finance Act 2026, section 7 (opens in a new tab) still sets the 2027 to 2028 property rates at 22%, 42% and 47%. The higher figures arrive only when Finance Bill 2026-27 becomes law.

Is mortgage interest relief changing too?

Yes, and in the landlord’s favour. You still can’t deduct mortgage interest as an expense. Instead you get a tax credit on it, and Schedule 1 of the Finance Act 2026 (opens in a new tab) has already changed that credit from the “basic rate” to the “property basic rate” from 2027 to 2028.

So the credit follows the property basic rate up. If Finance Bill 2026-27 sets that rate at 24% and leaves the link alone, your relief on interest rises from 20% to 24%. With Bank Rate at 4% (see our Bank Rate post), that matters. We’d call that the most useful detail for mortgaged landlords, and the one most headlines skip.

How much more tax will a Stoke-on-Trent landlord pay?

Less than the headline suggests, with a mortgage. The ONS private rent figures (opens in a new tab) published on 21 October put the average three-bedroom rent in Stoke-on-Trent at £800 a month in September 2026: £9,600 a year.

An illustration, not a tax calculation. Assume £3,000 a year of running costs (agent, repairs, insurance), leaving £6,600 of profit before interest, and £4,000 a year of mortgage interest:

LandlordTax in 2026 to 2027Tax in 2027 to 2028Change
Higher rate, £4,000 interest£1,840£1,944+£104
Higher rate, no mortgage£2,640£2,904+£264
Basic rate, £4,000 interest£520£624+£104
Basic rate, no mortgage£1,320£1,584+£264

The working for the first row: 40% of £6,600 is £2,640, less a 20% credit on £4,000 of interest (£800), gives £1,840. Next year it’s 44% of £6,600 (£2,904), less a 24% credit (£960), giving £1,944. The bigger credit absorbs part of the rise.

Why could the frozen tax bands matter more than the rate rise?

Because rents are rising and the bands are not. Section 10 of the Finance Act 2026 (opens in a new tab) keeps the personal allowance at £12,570 and the basic rate band at £37,700 up to and including 2030 to 2031. Meanwhile the ONS figures for the year to September 2026 show:

  • Stoke-on-Trent: average rent £718 a month, up 5.1%.
  • Newcastle-under-Lyme: £837, up 7.0%.
  • Staffordshire Moorlands: £732, up 7.6%.
  • West Midlands: £983, up 4.4%, against 4.0% for England.

If your salary or pension already uses most of the basic rate band, each rent rise pushes more of your rental profit into the 44% band. A 5% increase on a £9,600 rent is £480 more income. At the higher rate, £211 of that goes in tax.

Does the tax rise affect landlords with a limited company?

Not directly. The measure is aimed at individuals, partnerships and trusts. A company pays corporation tax on its rental profit, and the Finance Act 2026 (opens in a new tab) keeps the main rate at 25% for the year from April 2027, with the small profits rate at 19%. Dividend tax rates for 2027 to 2028 are unchanged at 10.75%, 35.75% and 39.35%, according to the Budget’s rates and allowances annex (opens in a new tab).

Does that make a company the answer? Not on its own. Moving property you already own into a company has tax costs of its own, and the answer depends on your income and borrowing. What should Staffordshire landlords do before April 2027?

  1. Work out your band for 2027 to 2028. Add your expected salary, pension and rental profit. From April 2027 your personal allowance is set against your other income first, under the ordering rules in HMRC’s technical note (opens in a new tab), so check where your rent lands.
  2. Price the change. Multiply your rental profit before interest by 4 points, then take off 4 points of your mortgage interest. That is roughly the extra bill.
  3. Keep every interest statement. The credit on interest is worth more from April 2027. Make sure you claim all of it.
  4. Claim every allowable cost. Repairs, agent fees and insurance reduce profit, and from April 2027 each £100 of cost saves £24, £44 or £49 of tax, depending on your band.
  5. Review your rent once a year, properly. Compare it with the ONS figures for your area and follow the Renters’ Rights Act process. Our Renters’ Rights Act guide covers how.
  6. Get your records digital. See our Making Tax Digital guide.
  7. Talk to an accountant before restructuring. Bring your numbers.

Questions landlords are asking

What are the new property income tax rates from April 2027?

Under Budget 2026, the property basic rate will be 24%, the property higher rate 44% and the property additional rate 49%, from the 2027 to 2028 tax year. They still need to be legislated in Finance Bill 2026-27.

When does the landlord tax rise start?

On 6 April 2027, the start of the 2027 to 2028 tax year. Rental profit for 2026 to 2027 is still taxed at the main rates of 20%, 40% and 45%.

How much more tax will landlords pay from April 2027?

Compared with 2026 to 2027, an extra £40 for every £1,000 of rental profit, in every band, less 4% of your mortgage interest if the interest credit rises to 24%. HMRC estimates the Budget 2026 rise alone will cost affected taxpayers an average of £225 a year in 2027 to 2028.

Does the property income tax rise apply to limited companies?

No. It applies to individuals, partnerships, trustees and personal representatives. Companies pay corporation tax, where the main rate stays at 25% for the year from April 2027.

Will I still get tax relief on my buy-to-let mortgage interest?

Yes, as a tax credit rather than a deduction. From 2027 to 2028 the law gives it at the property basic rate, so if that rate is set at 24%, the credit rises from 20% to 24%.
