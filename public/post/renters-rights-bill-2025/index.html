Renters' Rights Act 2025: What Changed for Landlords in May 2026

By GVN Estate Invest23 June 2025Updated 15 November 20267 min read

If you let a home in England, your tenancy agreements changed on 1 May 2026 whether you signed anything new or not. That was the day the first phase of the Renters’ Rights Act 2025 took effect, and it reached existing tenancies as well as new ones.

The short answer: since 1 May 2026, landlords in England can no longer use section 21, every assured tenancy is periodic, and rent can rise only once a year using Form 4A. To get a property back you now need a legal reason under section 8. Breaches of the new rules can bring council fines of up to £7,000.

Key points

  • Section 21 “no-fault” evictions ended for private tenancies in England on 1 May 2026, and court claims on older section 21 notices had to start by 31 July 2026 at the latest.
  • Every assured shorthold tenancy became an assured periodic tenancy on 1 May 2026, and tenants can leave with two months’ notice.
  • Under the Renters’ Rights Act, rent can rise once a year using Form 4A with at least two months’ notice, and a tenant can challenge the increase at the First-tier Tribunal for a £47 fee.
  • Landlords had to give existing tenants the government’s Renters’ Rights Act Information Sheet by 31 May 2026 or risk a fine of up to £7,000.
  • Council civil penalties now reach £7,000 for breaches and £40,000 for offences, under the government’s civil penalties guidance (opens in a new tab), and rent repayment orders can cover up to two years’ rent.

What is the Renters’ Rights Act 2025?

The Renters’ Rights Act 2025 is the law that reformed private renting in England, replacing fixed-term assured shorthold tenancies with rolling assured periodic tenancies. It received Royal Assent on 27 October 2025, and the government’s implementation roadmap (opens in a new tab) brings it in over three phases. The tenancy changes were switched on by the Commencement No. 2 Regulations 2026 (opens in a new tab) from 1 May 2026.

What changed for landlords on 1 May 2026?

On 1 May 2026, the Renters’ Rights Act changed how tenancies run and end. GOV.UK’s overview for landlords (opens in a new tab) puts the core change simply: “You cannot evict tenants using the section 21 process and will need a legal reason to evict them.” The rules that moved most:

Is section 21 abolished, and what replaced it?

Yes. Section 21 was abolished for private tenancies in England on 1 May 2026, and a landlord seeking possession now has to use section 8. A section 21 notice let a landlord end an assured shorthold tenancy without giving a reason; a section 8 notice (now Form 3A) names a legal ground, such as arrears, a sale or a move back in.

Notices served before 1 May had a short afterlife. GOV.UK’s guidance on notices given before 1 May 2026 (opens in a new tab) says court proceedings had to start by whichever came first: the time left on the notice, or 31 July 2026. Claims issued by then carry on.

Selling or moving yourself or a family member in usually needs four months’ notice and cannot be used in the first 12 months of a tenancy. The mandatory arrears ground in Schedule 2 to the Housing Act 1988 (opens in a new tab) now needs at least three months’ rent unpaid, both when the notice is served and at the hearing. With no fallback, a claim rests on evidence: a rent ledger, inspection notes, letters sent.

How do rent increases work under the Renters’ Rights Act?

Under the Renters’ Rights Act, a landlord can raise the rent once a year by serving Form 4A with at least two months’ notice, and not in the first 12 months of a new tenancy. A tenant who thinks the new rent is above the open market rent can ask the First-tier Tribunal to decide. According to GOV.UK’s page on applying for an open market rent determination (opens in a new tab), the application costs £47 and must be made before the new rent starts. The tribunal can set a figure lower than, or the same as, your proposed rent.

So the figure on your Form 4A needs to stand up. The Office for National Statistics (ONS) Price Index of Private Rents (opens in a new tab), published on 21 October 2026, puts the average rent in Stoke-on-Trent at £718 a month in September 2026, up 5.1% on a year earlier. Across the West Midlands the average was £983, up 4.4%, against 4.0% for England. We’d start there, then check what similar homes nearby actually let for.

What other rules started on 1 May 2026?

Four more rules for landlords and letting agents in England started on 1 May 2026:

  • No rental bidding. You must state an asking rent and cannot accept offers above it.
  • Limits on rent in advance. You cannot take rent before the tenancy agreement is signed, and GOV.UK’s overview for tenants (opens in a new tab) says a tenant can be asked for a maximum of one month’s rent in advance.
  • No discrimination. You cannot turn down tenants because they receive benefits or have children.
  • Pets. Section 11 of the Act (opens in a new tab) says consent “is not to be unreasonably refused”, and you must answer a request in writing within 28 days. In Staffordshire, Lichfield District Council’s guidance for landlords (opens in a new tab) adds that landlords “cannot require tenants to take out pet insurance, or charge tenants for it.”

What did landlords have to give tenants by 31 May 2026?

Landlords with written tenancies that began before 1 May 2026 had to give their tenants the Renters’ Rights Act Information Sheet by 31 May 2026. GOV.UK’s Information Sheet page (opens in a new tab) is blunt: miss it and “you could be fined up to £7,000.” A printed copy or the PDF attached to an email or text counts; a link to the PDF does not. Verbal tenancies needed a written summary of the main terms instead. Keep a record of how and when each copy went out.

What comes next after Phase 1?

Phase 2 of the Renters’ Rights Act is the landlord database, which GOV.UK says (opens in a new tab) opens on 15 December 2026 at £65 per property per year, with the West Midlands, including Stoke-on-Trent and Staffordshire, first. Our Phase 2 guide for West Midlands landlords covers registering step by step.

The roadmap expects landlord ombudsman membership to become compulsory in 2028. The Decent Homes Standard will apply to private rentals from 2035 (opens in a new tab). The government’s consultation on extending Awaab’s Law (opens in a new tab) to private rentals (deadlines to investigate and fix hazards such as damp and mould) closes on 18 December 2026.

For the tax side of 2026, see our post on section 21 and Making Tax Digital.

Questions landlords are asking

Can a landlord still use a section 21 notice?

No. Section 21 was abolished for private tenancies in England on 1 May 2026. A notice served before that date could only be used if court proceedings started by 31 July 2026, or sooner if the notice ran out first. Landlords now need a legal ground under section 8, served on Form 3A, to seek possession.

How often can a landlord raise the rent under the Renters’ Rights Act?

Once a year. The landlord must serve Form 4A with at least two months’ notice, and cannot raise the rent in the first 12 months of a new tenancy. A tenant who thinks the new rent is above the open market rent can apply to the First-tier Tribunal for £47 before the new rent starts.

How much notice does a tenant have to give to leave?

Two months. Since 1 May 2026, every assured tenancy in England is periodic, with no fixed end date, so a tenant can end it at any time by giving the landlord two months’ notice. Landlords can only end a tenancy using a legal ground for possession under section 8.

Can a landlord refuse a tenant’s request to keep a pet?

Only for a reasonable reason. The Renters’ Rights Act says consent to keep a pet must not be unreasonably refused, and the landlord must reply in writing within 28 days of the request. Lichfield District Council gives examples such as a property too small for the animal or a lease that bans pets.
