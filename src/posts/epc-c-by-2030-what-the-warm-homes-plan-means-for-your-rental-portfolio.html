Ten thousand pounds. That is the most a landlord in England or Wales would have to spend on a rented home under the government’s plan to lift the private rented sector to EPC band C by 1 October 2030. The plan is confirmed. It is not yet law.

The short answer: the government confirmed on 21 January 2026 that privately rented homes in England and Wales must reach EPC band C by 1 October 2030, with required spending capped at £10,000 a property. The rules still need an Act of Parliament and new regulations. Until then, the legal minimum stays at band E.

Key points

  • The government’s response of 21 January 2026 sets one compliance date, 1 October 2030, for every private tenancy in England and Wales, with no earlier date for new lets.
  • Landlords would have to spend up to £10,000 per property; for homes worth under £100,000, the cap is 10% of the value. Spending from 1 October 2025 counts.
  • The legal minimum today is still EPC band E, with a £3,500 cost cap and fines of up to £5,000 per property.
  • The Energy Independence Bill, introduced on 4 November 2026, would raise the maximum fine to £30,000. It is a Bill, not law.
  • ONS figures for EPCs lodged in the ten years to March 2025 show 45.3% of homes in Stoke-on-Trent at band C or above, against 50.0% for the West Midlands and 53.3% for England.

Is the EPC C rule for rented homes law yet?

The EPC C rule for private rented homes is confirmed government policy, not yet law. An Energy Performance Certificate (EPC) rates a home’s energy efficiency from A to G. The Minimum Energy Efficiency Standard (MEES) is the lowest rating a landlord may legally let at, set by the Energy Efficiency (Private Rented Property) (England and Wales) Regulations 2015 (opens in a new tab).

The Department for Energy Security and Net Zero’s response, published on 21 January 2026 (opens in a new tab), says the changes are “subject to Parliamentary approval”. The government will seek new powers by Act of Parliament, then lay regulations, which it aimed to bring into force in 2027. The first step arrived on 4 November 2026, when the Energy Independence Bill was introduced to Parliament (opens in a new tab).

Which rented homes have to reach EPC C, and by when?

Every privately rented home in England and Wales covered by the regulations would have to reach band C by 1 October 2030, whether the tenancy is new or long-running. The response is explicit: “There will not be an earlier compliance date for new tenancies.” The 2025 consultation had proposed 2028 for new tenancies and 2030 for the rest.

There is one useful carve-out. A home that scores C or higher on the current Energy Efficiency Rating, on an EPC lodged before 1 October 2029, will count as compliant until that EPC expires or is replaced. Short-term lets are not in scope for now, though the government says it is keeping that under review.

How much will landlords have to spend to reach EPC C?

Landlords would have to spend up to £10,000 per property, and the government’s impact assessment estimates the average spend at £5,400 once the cap is applied. The 2025 consultation had proposed £15,000. If a home still falls short after the full spend, the landlord can register a cost-cap exemption lasting 10 years.

Three details are worth knowing:

  • Spending from 1 October 2025 counts towards the cap, so keep invoices for work already done.
  • Grants and other third-party funding count, except Boiler Upgrade Scheme grants.
  • EPCs commissioned before and after improvement works can be included in the cap. An EPC bought for any other reason can’t.

The lower cap is a live question in Stoke-on-Trent. HM Land Registry’s UK House Price Index (opens in a new tab) put the average Stoke-on-Trent flat or maisonette at £89,610 in August 2026, and the average terraced house at £127,868. On the 10% rule, a flat worth £90,000 would face a cap of £9,000, not £10,000.

What will EPC C be measured on?

EPC C will be measured on two new metrics: a primary standard on fabric performance, and a secondary standard on either the heating system or smart readiness, at the landlord’s choice. Fabric means the building itself, such as its insulation and glazing. The response asks landlords to put fabric first, so the home needs less energy before the heating is changed.

These metrics will appear on reformed EPCs produced with the Home Energy Model, the government’s new method for rating homes, which will replace the Standard Assessment Procedure (SAP) (opens in a new tab). The January response aimed to deliver new EPCs from October 2026. On 10 March 2026 the government moved the launch to the second half of 2027 (opens in a new tab). New-format EPCs are not available yet, and the band boundaries for each metric are to be confirmed once the model is complete.

What should landlords do about EPC C now?

Landlords should use the time before the rules become law to find out where each property stands. We’d read the January response as a planning document: the date and cap are firm intentions, but the detail could still move in Parliament.

  1. Check every certificate. Look up each rating and expiry date on the GOV.UK Find an energy certificate (opens in a new tab) service.
  2. Note which homes are already C. An EPC rated C on the current scale and lodged before 1 October 2029 would count until it expires or is replaced.
  3. Keep every invoice from 1 October 2025. Qualifying work since then counts towards the cap.
  4. Plan fabric work first. The new standard makes fabric the primary measure, with heating or smart readiness second.
  5. Fold it into wider plans. If a refurbishment is due anyway, see our buy, refurbish, refinance, repeat guide. If you’d rather sell than upgrade, read our capital gains tax guide first.

Questions landlords are asking

Is EPC C for rental properties law yet?

No. The government confirmed on 21 January 2026 that privately rented homes in England and Wales must reach EPC band C by 1 October 2030, but it needs new powers by Act of Parliament and new regulations first. The legal minimum today is still band E under the 2015 Regulations, with a cost cap of £3,500.

When do rented homes need to reach EPC C?

Under the confirmed plan, every private tenancy in England and Wales must meet band C by 1 October 2030. There is no earlier date for new tenancies. A home rated C on the current scale, on an EPC lodged before 1 October 2029, counts as compliant until that certificate expires or is replaced.

How much will landlords have to spend to reach EPC C?

Up to £10,000 per property under the plan, or 10% of the value for homes worth less than £100,000. Spending from 1 October 2025 counts towards the cap. The government’s impact assessment estimates the average spend at £5,400 per property once the cap is applied.

What happens if a rental property can’t reach EPC C within the cap?

If a landlord spends up to the cap and the home still falls short of band C, the landlord can register a cost-cap exemption under the plan. That exemption would last 10 years, compared with 5 years for the cost-cap exemption under today’s rules. Most other exemptions would still last 5 years.
